They're Coming for the Beans

On the price of a fry-up, the egg cliff of 2023, and what gets measured when nobody's officially watching.

There is a particular cafe — you know the one — where the man behind the counter says nothing as he places it in front of you. Two eggs, two sausages, two rashers, mushrooms, beans, fried bread, slice of black pudding, grilled tomato, brown sauce on the side, fork already on the table. The cup of tea is automatic. The price, until quite recently, was somewhere between £6 and £7, and felt like one of the unbreakable laws of British economics — a reliable line, like cricket in the summer, or the rain in Manchester. Then it became £8. Then £8.50. And in some places that I will not name out of professional charity, £10.

The fry-up has been silently inflating for five years and almost nobody has been writing it up. The Office for National Statistics has been reporting on "food and non-alcoholic beverages" with great honesty and great regularity. They have not been reporting on the plate, because the plate is not their unit of measurement. Nobody's is. So we built one.

The new English Breakfast Index on the dashboard tracks the eight ingredients of a proper full English — bacon, eggs, sausages, black pudding, mushrooms, vine tomatoes, baked beans, white bread — with their pack prices and their per-plate portion costs. The companion card, the Fry-Up Tracker, walks each ingredient backwards through five years of ONS food sub-category CPI to show how each line got here. The numbers are sobering. The verdict line, when the cumulative gets bad enough, reads: They're coming for the beans. It is meant as a joke. It is mostly a joke.

I. The £4.40 Plate

Pull a full English apart at a mid-tier UK supermarket today and the per-plate cost lands at £4.40. That number was £3.12 five years ago, in 2021. The plate is up £1.28, or roughly 41%, over five years — and that is not "above inflation" in some abstract sense. The ONS top-line CPI moved about a quarter over the same period. The fry-up moved nearly twice that. The British breakfast has been outpacing the British economy.

The trick of putting it on a plate, rather than in a basket, is that the plate is something a human being can hold. A 4.3% YoY food-CPI print is a press release. £4.40 is a Saturday morning. It is also, importantly, the home version. Out at the cafe — where you also pay rent, gas, wages, and a small contribution to the new Costa over the road — that £4.40 plate is somewhere north of £8, and rising. The man behind the counter is not greedy. The man behind the counter is desperate.

II. The Egg Cliff

The line on the Fry-Up Tracker does not move evenly. It does what real cost-of-living moves do, which is sit quietly for a few years and then jump off a cliff. There is a calm 2021 — bird flu was still scattered, gas was cheap, Britain had not yet noticed it was hungry. Then 2022 happens. Eggs jumped 22% in a single year. Bread followed at 12%. By the end of 2023 — the peak year, when food inflation overall hit 19.2% YoY — eggs were up nearly 40% on 2021, bread was up a third, and the Spanish growers had run out of water. There was, briefly, a national rationing of vine tomatoes that the supermarkets handled with the dignity of an unprepared Year 9 substitute teacher.

Five years on, the worst offenders are exactly the ingredients you would expect, and the steady ones are exactly the ingredients nobody got worked up about:

There was no Sunday Times investigation into mushroom prices. Nobody wrote a column titled The Mushroom Shock. There were no riots in the Asda fungi aisle. Mushrooms quietly held the line, did their job, and ended the half-decade up a respectable but unremarkable 20%. The black pudding line on the chart is even flatter — the Bury manufacturers, having spent decades being a national in-joke, have apparently stopped being moved by world events. The cured-pork-and-oats sector takes its temperature from a kettle, not from Bloomberg.

Eggs, though. Eggs did the work. If you were going to pick the single ingredient most responsible for the British grocery shopper's sense, in late 2022, that the country had broken — it would be eggs. Bread is the policy story. Eggs are the cultural one. There was a moment, somewhere around January 2023, when the egg shelves at certain supermarkets were genuinely empty, with hand-written signs apologising for it. It is a photograph that will go in the textbooks: a country that had once led the world in industrial poultry standing in front of a refrigerated display unit with a bit of A4 saying Sorry — eggs.

The fry-up moved nearly twice as much as the economy. The British breakfast has been outpacing the British economy.

III. The Bean Line

The verdict line under the Fry-Up Tracker, when the cumulative gets bad enough, reads "They're coming for the beans." The line escalates. There is a calm "Plate's holding" at the bottom and a "This is now a war" at the top, with milestones at "Bacon's being cheeky" and "The beans have fallen" along the way. We wrote the line as a joke. We have spent the last five years watching it slowly become a fact.

Beans are up 37% since 2021. Heinz are up 37%. Branston are up 37%. The own-brand alternative, that dependable supermarket fixture, is up 37%. Beans, like eggs, like bread, are the kind of grocery you don't price-check, because their price has — until recently — been a sort of natural constant. The thing about natural constants is that when they move, they tend to move all at once, and people only notice when the change crosses some unwritten line of indecency. For beans, that line was probably 99p. They are now £1.10. We have crossed it. They are coming.

The serious version of this argument is that there is something profoundly democratic about putting cost-of-living data on the plate where it lives. The ONS basket is real and the index is real and nothing on this site is a substitute for it. But the ONS is not, on any given Tuesday, in a position to tell you that a full English now costs £4.40 at home and somewhere north of £8 out. They do not work in those units. They work in food sub-categories — bread and cereals, meat, milk and eggs — and report cumulative percentages that read like financial data rather than groceries. This is partly fine. It is also a reason that the egg cliff of 2023 happened in real time, was correctly described in the official tools, and was nonetheless absorbed by the British public as a personal grievance about their shop, on their Saturday, rather than as a national event.

IV. What's Worth Counting

If you take the long view, the dashboard's Fry-Up Tracker is doing one specific job: it counts what the ONS does not count, in the unit a person actually shops in, and it does so in public. It is not a substitute for serious economic measurement. It is a translator. The egg line is the same egg line that the ONS reports — the source data is the same, the magnitudes are the same. We have just plotted it on a chart that you can read while you eat your breakfast.

I think there is a small civic value in this. The American sociologist Erving Goffman, who I quoted in the last essay and who I will keep quoting until people stop letting me, wrote that the deepest secrets of a society are not in what it talks about, but in what it doesn't bother to. We do not talk about the price of beans. We do not need to. Everyone already knows. So — borderline indecently — we put it on a chart. Out loud. With colours. With a verdict line that escalates from quiet grumble to "this is now a war."

You can read the chart as a joke. You can read the £4.40 number as a fact about your weekend. You can read the verdict line as either, and probably should read it as both at once. They are, simultaneously, the same thing — which is the point. Britain is measured by what Britain pays for its breakfast, and Britain has been quietly paying more for years. Now, finally, it has a number.

Have a brew. Mind the bacon. Watch the beans.

The Stella Index, the Cocaine in the Thames, and Why I Built a Dashboard for the Vibe

A short defence of measuring the unmeasurable — and a longer one of why every nation should have a national mood-ring, ideally one calibrated to whether its corner shop has run out of curry sauce.

There is a particular feeling, familiar to anyone raised in this country, that arrives somewhere between half-five and seven o'clock on a Friday in late summer. It is the feeling that something is, technically, about to happen. The street has more people on it than usual. Two of them are walking abreast. There are bags. Someone is on the phone in a tone that is louder than it needs to be, and a third party — let's call him Lee — has not yet arrived but has texted "5 mins" a quarter of an hour ago. The pubs are at that very specific stage of fullness: not busy, exactly, but you'd struggle to find a chair without making a friend. A police van has just been parked, casually, somewhere it didn't need to be. And the thing is, you can feel all of this without registering any of it. You just walk into a Sainsbury's Local for a Fanta and emerge muttering, "It's a bit kicking off out there, isn't it."

I have spent some years professionally interested in this exact threshold — the moment when a city, a town, a nation, decides that today is going to be a day. Most countries don't really have it, or have it at a much higher amplitude. America has riots; we have "a few of them have got their tops off near the bus station, lock the door." Italy has politics; we have a man with a Stella who has decided the council are not going to get away with this any longer. The British register of impending disorder is a permanent low hum, almost beneath conscious perception, and every English person carries a small and very accurate detector for it that they do not know they are carrying. We pretend it is just intuition. It is not. It is data — about a hundred ambient signals fused together, processed in milliseconds, and reported as a feeling.

So the question, which a friend asked me at exactly the kind of pub I am describing, was: could you measure it?

I. The Sociology of the Brew

The trick, as Rory Sutherland likes to point out, is that human behaviour is mostly governed by signals so peripheral that the system pretending to govern us — economics, policy, deterrence — does not even know they are there. We do not decide to leave the house because of a cost-benefit analysis about social opportunity. We decide because the kettle has just clicked off. The kettle is the policy lever. If you wanted to reduce binge drinking in Britain, you would not do it through pricing. You would do it by changing what's on the telly at 9 pm on a Friday. The ITV listings page is a more powerful instrument of national policy than the entire Department for Culture, Media and Sport, and nobody at DCMS has ever realised this.

The dashboard you are reading the blog of is, in this sense, less a piece of comedy than a piece of sociology pretending to be a piece of comedy. It tracks roughly thirty live signals — the actual fixtures of clubs whose travelling support is on the Met's quiet list; the Open-Meteo temperature in Manchester and Cardiff; the rolling jackpot at the National Lottery; today's RSS-aggregated tone of the British press; the National Grid's electricity demand (the famous post-EastEnders kettle spike is on this, and it is glorious); the live menu of your nearest Spoons, indirectly, via a model of UK pub-rhythm; the GDELT global news-tone API filtered to UK protest, strike, riot and unrest keywords. None of these signals, in isolation, mean anything. Together, they correlate weirdly well with the answer to the question of whether your nan wants to walk home through town tonight or not.

Some of the signals are entirely live: the BBC headlines, the gov.uk threat level, the police data feed for Central London phone snatches, the Met Office wind. Some are modelled — meaning we have made a careful, calibrated guess based on real published numbers. The Stella Artois Index, for example, is calibrated to roughly 2.5 million pints poured on a peak Saturday across the UK, with hour-of-day, day-of-week, and bank-holiday modifiers. Is this number "true"? No. Is it more true than not knowing? Almost certainly. The actual brewery does not publish its hourly UK throughput, and even if it did, the version on this site would still be funnier.

II. Why a Brit Was Always Going to Build This

You could not, I think, build a serious version of this dashboard in another country. The Americans would put a flag on it and pretend it was a homeland-security tool. The French would put a Gauloise on it and add an ennui meter. The Germans would build it correctly, document it exhaustively in a 400-page PDF, and be terribly disappointed when nobody clicked through. The British genius, such as it is, is in noticing that the threshold between a Tuesday and a kick-off is partly a matter of how much rain has fallen since lunch, and that this is not just a joke — it is, quite literally, the operating model of the country.

We are a nation made anxious by the wrong things and not at all by the right ones. We are exquisitely sensitive to whether the postman has been but blissfully unbothered by the long-term solvency of the state pension. We will form a queue to register a complaint about the queue. We have a national broadcaster that, between 6 pm and 8 pm on most evenings, carries forty minutes of weather forecasts inside thirty minutes of news. The national mood is not really up or down. It is calibrated to something in between, and that something is approximately the average ambient temperature of a Wetherspoons in Doncaster on a Wednesday — a value somewhere around "could be worse", with bank-holiday-driven excursions into the genuinely interesting in either direction.

The British register of impending disorder is a permanent low hum, almost beneath conscious perception, and every English person carries a small and very accurate detector for it that they do not know they are carrying.

There is a real argument, which I will only briefly sketch, that being able to name this national feeling is a small civic kindness. The American sociologist Erving Goffman wrote that the deepest secrets of a society are not in what it talks about, but in what it doesn't bother to. We do not bother to talk about the Stella Index. We do not need to. Everyone already knows. So — borderline indecently — we put it on a dashboard. Out loud. With an emoji. With a needle that swings slowly from BREW at the bottom, through NICKED and ROWDY, all the way up to ALL OUT SCRAP at the top. It is, I think, the first time in British history a 0–100 number has been painted onto the national mood, and the mood has visibly approved.

III. Cocaine in the Thames, and Other Modern Truths

Nothing on the dashboard cheers me up quite like the Cocaine in UK Waters module. It is modelled — there is no live wastewater feed, alas — but the model is calibrated to a real 2019 King's College London study that found cocaine in 100% of Thames shrimp, and to the established weekend-Monday wastewater-purge cycle that European drug-monitoring agencies report year on year. It is, in short, both a joke and absolutely true. The Thames carries, on a Sunday morning, a measurable concentration of the previous night's national misadventure. That fact — that one of the great rivers of Europe is provably cokey — should, by rights, be in every Year Six geography textbook. It is not. So we put it on a dashboard.

I am increasingly of the view that almost everything important about a country is in this register: it is provably true, mildly comic, slightly depressing, faintly inspiring, and the official channels will never tell you about it. The official channels will tell you about quarterly inflation prints and ONS housing starts. They will not tell you that the McFlurry machine at Bristol Temple Meads McDonald's has been broken for nine days. They will not tell you that on a hot Friday in July the kebab shops of Manchester collectively process more meat-by-volume than the average Premier League ground processes burgers across an entire season. They will not tell you that on Bonfire Night the National Grid reports a measurable but small dip in domestic demand at the moment local fireworks displays peak — because everyone is briefly outside, in the cold, watching a wet rocket fail to clear a tree.

These are the things I want my dashboard for. Not because they tell us how to live — they don't — but because they tell us, in a register no other instrument has, that we are alive, in this country, in this hour, in this weather. That is, in the end, what a national mood-ring is for. Some days you look at it and the needle is at 12. Some days it is at 78 and someone has called Lee and Lee is on his way. The dashboard does not change either of those facts. It just notices them, in passing, and gives them a number, and a colour, and a tier name like NICKED or DEFCON BREW, and lets you see that you were not imagining it.

You weren't, by the way. It really is a bit kicking off. Have a brew. Mind how you go.
