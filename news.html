Vote Burnham, Get Starmer Out

On the post-election clown show, the Makerfield paradox, and what the gauge actually saw the country do.

For about a year, the principal slogan of the Reform UK campaign — printed on billboards, painted on the side of a coach, repeated on hundreds of doorsteps and a great many GB News panels — was, in one form or another: Vote Reform, get Starmer out. It was a clean line. It was a stupid line. It was, like most of British politics this decade, the kind of line that turns out to be roughly true while being roughly incorrect.

If you want to remove Sir Keir Starmer from the office of Prime Minister this June, you will not be voting Reform UK. You will not even be voting Conservative. You will be voting Labour, in a by-election in Makerfield, for Andy Burnham — who has emerged this week as the only sitting British politician with both a chance of unseating the man currently in Downing Street and a small army of Mancunians who actually like him.

It is a peculiar moment.

I. The Clown Show Opens

Two weeks ago, Britain went to the polls in the 2026 locals. It was, by any historical measure, a strange ballot. Labour — the governing party — collapsed to 15% in the National Equivalent Vote, the joint-lowest figure Ipsos has ever recorded for the party. Reform UK won 27% and a generation of Tory shire councils to go with it. The Greens, under their newish, sharper-edged leader Zack Polanski, held up at 14%. The Liberal Democrats quietly hoovered up the Home Counties on 14% as well, achieving the rare distinction of being everyone's third or fourth choice while still ending the night smiling. The Conservatives finished on 20% and, on Friday morning, did the only thing the Conservative Party knows how to do reliably, which was to call it a victory in the local press while leaking that the leader is in trouble.

The result was, on the night, a Reform-shaped landslide. By Saturday morning, it had become something messier — a four-way fragmentation that no British general election system has any idea what to do with. The two-party adversarial mechanism, which has run the country since approximately 1922, has officially broken down. We have, in its place, five medium-sized factions, none of whom can deliver a clean majority in any plausible electoral configuration.

By Tuesday — five days after results — Sir Keir Starmer's net favourability had slipped to −50.

A Prime Minister cannot reasonably govern a country with a net favourability of −50. He can exist in office at that rating. He can give speeches, host dinners, table legislation, do the Today programme. But he cannot, in any politically meaningful sense, lead. The party knew this on Friday morning. The party briefed it to the lobby on Friday afternoon. By Tuesday, it was on the front of every paper. By Thursday, an MP had resigned a safe seat to clear a runway for a challenger.

II. The Makerfield Paradox

The challenger is Andy Burnham. He is the Mayor of Greater Manchester. He is not currently a sitting Member of Parliament, which is awkward, because Labour party rules require any leadership candidate to be a sitting member of the parliamentary Labour party. This is a rule that nobody minds in normal times and which becomes the load-bearing wall of the whole crisis in peculiar times.

To get Burnham into Parliament, a sitting Labour MP — Josh Simons, of Makerfield — has helpfully resigned his seat. The Labour NEC has approved Burnham as the candidate. A by-election will follow in June. If Burnham wins it (he probably will; it is a 2024-vintage Labour safe seat in the North-West, and he is the most popular Labour-aligned politician in the country), he becomes a Labour MP. At which point he can table a leadership challenge. At which point Starmer, in all likelihood, falls.

Let us absorb the route the British political system has just produced.

To remove the leader of the Labour Party from Downing Street, the country requires the resignation of a Labour MP, in a Labour safe seat, so a Labour mayor can win a Labour by-election as a Labour candidate, in order to mount a leadership challenge inside the Labour Party.

Reform UK has, in the meantime, gone very quiet on the "get Starmer out" slogan. They are no longer the vehicle by which Starmer is removed. The vehicle, it turns out, is Andy Burnham in Wigan. Nigel Farage cannot vote for him. Most Reform voters cannot vote for him either, because they don't live in Makerfield. The only people who can vote to evict the Prime Minister, this round, are a few thousand people in a constituency that voted Labour by eighteen points in 2024 and which has, until last Wednesday, been minding its own business.

This is what political scientists, when pressed for a technical term, will call a clown show.

III. What the Gauge Actually Saw

You would expect, all of this being so plainly kicking off, that our dashboard would have a story to tell. And it does, partly.

We pulled the last two weeks of snapshots from the gauge — about a thousand readings, captured roughly every twenty minutes between 5 and 18 May — and laid them next to the calendar.

The peak of the entire fortnight came at 22:00 on Saturday 9 May, when the gauge hit 77 and tripped, for the only time in the whole window, into the IT'S KICKING OFF tier. That is the post-election Saturday — papers landing, Sunday columns being filed, Labour digesting the scale of defeat, Reform putting a new sign on the door.

The second mountain in the data was Saturday 16 May, the day of the Unite the Kingdom rally — sixty thousand or so of Tommy Robinson's supporters in central London, plus a pro-Palestinian counter-march of comparable size. The gauge hit the mid-60s on Saturday evening and spent Sunday with its protests signal at 0.669, the second-highest protest reading of the whole window.

These are two real things. Two real events that involved real people in real public space, doing real political business. The gauge saw them and reported them accordingly.

The gauge did not see the leadership challenge.

Through the period 12–15 May — the days when Burnham was emerging in the lobby copy, when Josh Simons was resigning, when the NEC was approving — the daily mean gauge score actually fell. The news signal declined. The voting polarisation reading declined. Even the leaders signal, which is the one signal you'd think would have a Burnham bump, stayed flat — pegged at the elevated 0.89–0.91 level it has been at since results night.

This is not a malfunction. It is the dashboard, working as designed, telling the truth.

IV. What the Gauge is Telling Us

The dashboard was built — and we've written this elsewhere — to measure the texture of public mood. Stella consumption, chip shops, kettle pressure, the news cycle, the tutting volume of the train carriage at 8am. It does not measure Westminster's mood. Westminster has its own mood — measured by political correspondents on Sky News, by lobby briefings, and by the daily fluctuations of the futures market on Polymarket. That mood is a different climate. It runs on different weather.

The fact that the British public was not kicking off between 12 and 15 May — even as the Prime Minister of the country was being quite visibly hollowed out — tells us something true and slightly uncomfortable, which is that Britain is not, at this exact moment, panicking about its government.

This is in part a function of Westminster being far away from the kebab shop. It is in part a function of fatigue: we have been through five Conservative prime ministers in the past decade, and the eviction of a sixth, even a Labour one, registers as more administrative than tragic. And it is in part — let us be honest — a function of the country having quietly, on 7 May, already voted on the question. They voted, decisively, "not these people, no, thank you." The Westminster machine is now going through the various procedural motions of catching up with the country. The country, having said its piece, has gone back to the chip shop.

The gauge knows this. The gauge does not light up when a Prime Minister is being eased out by his own party in a small Wigan constituency. The gauge lights up when sixty thousand people march through Whitehall.

V. Is It Kicking Off?

In the specific, dashboard-defined sense — is the country currently registering high aggravation? — the answer is no. Today's reading is sitting around 22, HAVING A BREW. Yesterday it was 30. The day before, 36. We are, as a nation, currently quite calm. We had our shout on the 7th, we got our protest in on the 16th, and the rest is for Westminster to sort out.

In the larger, allegorical sense — is something kicking off in Britain right now? — the answer is obviously, yes, but quietly. The clown show in the Labour Party is the surface manifestation of something deeper: the steady, almost geological decoupling of a political class from a country that has stopped recognising itself in it. Reform's 27%, Labour's 15%, the Lib Dems' Home Counties haul, the Greens' urban surge, and Andy Burnham's Wigan path-to-Number-10 are all symptoms of the same underlying thing — a country that no longer believes the Westminster theatre is the one running the show.

The clown show is what happens when the theatre forgets it is on stage. The gauge, mercifully, is in the stalls. Sometimes you have to be in the stalls to see what is actually happening.

The bins, in the meantime, keep coming. The kettle is on. The gauge is currently sitting at HAVING A BREW.

It Used to Be About Bins

On the new election tracker under the gauge, the uni-party, voter apathy, the Greens, Reform's surge, and what Yes Minister got right about polling day.

Today, we added a new module under the main gauge: an Election Day Tracker. It is — for the next week, at least — the noisiest thing on the page. It pulls live election headlines from the BBC, the Guardian, Sky, and the Mirror; filters them for the keywords any sensible British citizen now scrolls past with a sigh (uni-party, swing, vote share, manifesto, Polanski, Farage, Starmer); and runs a small polarisation meter against the lot. It does not, deliberately, contribute to the main aggravation score. It is an observer, not a participant. We did not want a polling-day spike to flatter or punish the gauge in either direction. We just wanted to watch.

Specifically, we wanted to watch the next seven days. Polls open tomorrow at 7am, and somewhere in the small hours of Friday morning the country will discover that several of its municipal councils have flipped — some to Reform, some to the Greens, some to nobody very much in particular. The dashboard's job, this week, is to register the quiet bit. The bit between the headline and the result. The bit where a country that thinks of itself as moderate slowly notices, in the texture of its own news cycle, that it isn't moderate any more. We are watching for polarisation. We are watching, frankly, to see whether the country still pretends it isn't divided.

Because the country is divided. Stupidly, theatrically, sectarianly — it is divided in a way the British political vocabulary doesn't quite have the words for yet. We have language for "left" and "right." We have language for "remain" and "leave." We have less language, somehow, for the more recent thing: a country in which five medium-sized factions all believe the others are: a) lying, b) extreme, and c) about to wreck everything.

There used to be a sentence the British said about local elections, and it went like this: it's about the bins. You voted for whoever had the better record on the bins. The bins came every Wednesday, or they didn't; the council had spent the parking-fine windfall sensibly, or it had spent it on a sculpture; the leisure centre was open at 6am or it wasn't. You voted accordingly. You went home. You did not, as a general rule, weep about it.

That sentence is dying. It is being replaced by another sentence, which goes: it's a referendum on the prime minister. Tomorrow the country will, in theory, vote for which party of councillors should oversee the bins of England. In practice, the country will be voting on what it thinks of Sir Keir Starmer — the man who, eighteen months in, has become the thing the British political system always does to its prime ministers, which is to first make him a saviour, and then make him a punchline, and then leave him in the kind of polling territory that the SDP-Liberal Alliance briefly enjoyed in 1987 before disappearing into the historical equivalent of a damp squib.

I. The Bin Election

Local elections in this country have been, for some years, a national mood-ring administered by the bin men. The country uses them, half-knowingly, to register a feeling about the government. The government, half-knowingly, treats the result as predictive of a general election that may or may not come. The councillors, knowing all of this, hold their nerve and try not to be seen on doorsteps in the immediate aftermath.

There is something almost Yes-Minister-ian about this — the polite, slightly stunned acceptance that the country has, in the last eighteen months, fragmented into something that looks like Italy used to. The polite consensus that ran British politics for thirty years has been replaced by five medium-sized factions. The two-party system used to deliver one party at 42% and another at 38%, and a Lib Dem on Question Time. The current system delivers four parties bunched between 13% and 28%, and a fifth — the Greens — staking everything on the cities and the angrier seminar rooms.

II. Three Flavours of Disgust

The polarised version of British politics is best understood as three flavours of disgust, each of them confidently, constantly, and quite tediously certain of the others' bad faith.

There is the Reform / Restore Britain disgust, which holds that the country has been quietly hijacked by a Liberal-Establishment-Civil-Service axis since approximately 1997, and that everyone except Reform UK is part of a "uni-party" — Labour and the Conservatives mashed together into a single managerial blob whose primary function is to run the country into the ground while seeming respectable on Newsnight. Reform's billboards, this week, say things like VOTE THE LOT OUT. Their poll numbers say things like 28%. The two facts are connected.

There is the Green disgust, which is the disgust of people who believe everyone to their right is in the pocket of fossil capital and everyone to their left has not read enough Marx. It has, in the last six months under Zack Polanski, shaded into a sharper-edged thing — a more confrontational left-populism that flirts with British Corbynism but with better social media. After the Golders Green row, Polanski's net favourability has fallen further than it climbed. The Greens are polling at 8%, which is, by the standards of Green Party history, riotously high. They will not win many seats tomorrow. They will, however, win the discourse of certain neighbourhoods quite comprehensively.

And there is the Uni-Party disgust, which is the disgust of the great tired centre — Labour and Conservative voters who watch each other's leaders tank in the YouGov tracker and feel a quiet, bipartisan grief. Starmer is on −45. Badenoch is on −21. Together they are, on paper, the British political establishment. Together they are also the most unloved establishment Britain has had since the late 1970s. Their disgust is mostly with the situation, and only secondarily with each other.

III. What Yes Minister Knew

Sir Humphrey, in 1981, would have understood tomorrow precisely. The genius of Yes Minister was that it was never really about politics — it was about the structural inertia of a system designed to absorb every reformist impulse and reshape it, gently, into a memo. Tomorrow's local elections will produce thousands of swung seats, three or four high-profile scalps, and approximately zero changes to the actual operation of British municipal life. The bins, where they were collected on Wednesday, will continue to be collected on Wednesday. The bins where they were not, will not.

The Thick of It updated this insight for the early 21st century, with louder swearing. Malcolm Tucker's Britain — the spin-room Britain, the Today Programme Britain, the leaked-text-message Britain — is now everyone's Britain, all the time, on TikTok, with worse angles. The political class is not in worse shape than in 2009. The amplification is.

IV. The Brexit Hangover, Continued

You cannot write about a British local election in 2026 without reckoning with the Brexit hangover, which is now in its ninth year and shows no sign of clearing. The Brexit referendum was, retrospectively, the moment a previously disengaged electorate discovered that voting did, occasionally, do something. It then proceeded to do quite a lot of things, most of them economic, most of them not what was promised on the side of a bus. The lesson the British public took from Brexit was not that voting works. It was that voting can work, and that when it does, the people in charge will tell you that it didn't, that it can't have, that it cannot possibly mean what you took it to mean. This is a politically corrosive lesson. It is also why Reform is at 28%.

V. The Polling Station at 21:58

There is a moment, every May, in every town and village hall in England, that is deeply, distinctively British. It is the two minutes before the polling station closes, when the last few voters drift in apologetically — having forgotten, having put it off, having had a row about it, having decided in the car that they ought to do their bit. They tick a box. They walk home. They go to bed. They wake up on Friday and find out they have re-elected the same councillor for the eleventh consecutive term.

Tomorrow's local elections will be many things. They will be a referendum on Starmer. They will be Reform's first proper test at scale. They will tell us whether the Polanski Greens have legs or just a Twitter. They will, in the small hours of Friday morning, reveal which English shires have swung and which English cities have fortified. Most of them, it should be said, will still be about the bins.

The new tracker, under the gauge, will be watching all of it — the headlines, the polarisation, the swing. It will not score it. It will just register it, quietly, until next Wednesday, when the news cycle moves on and the gauge goes back to measuring the country by its kettles, queues, and chip-shop tutting volume.

We will, of course, treat the national-barometer reading as if it is the only thing that matters. We are British. That is what we do.

The bins, mercifully, will keep coming. Polls open at 7. Don't be the person at 21:58.

What the Cousins Know

On Mar-a-Lago, the gator hotline, and why the United States is best read at swamp level.

There is a particular thing that happens to a country's political centre when the air conditioning gets too good. It drifts. New York had it for a while. Washington has it on paper. London, briefly, had it on the South Bank. But the actual centre — the place where willpower sits, where the people who make decisions go on Wednesdays in February — has, for the second time in a decade, settled on a pink stucco resort with a flagpole and a buffet, a few miles from a stretch of beach where a man was last week arrested for trying to baptise a small alligator.

The Florida Man Index, which has now joined the dashboard as a sister gauge to the British one, was originally a joke. Britain measures itself by the volume of activity in the chip shop queue at half eleven on a Saturday. America, increasingly, measures itself by what gets posted to r/FloridaMan at half eleven on a Tuesday. The two indicators are not the same kind. Britain's signal is class-mediated, ambient, and largely about volume. America's signal is climate-mediated, theatrical, and largely about deviation from the median. The British metric tells you whether things are going. The Florida metric tells you whether anyone is still in charge.

I. The Centre of Gravity Has Gone South

When a country's political class permanently relocates its centre of gravity to a state that contains both Disney World and the highest concentration of nuisance-gator calls per capita in the developed world, certain things follow. The first is that the country's seriousness is, by deliberate choice, framed in a setting that includes ride photographs. The second is that the local fauna becomes, by gradual default, a sort of atmospheric chorus to the political news. Mar-a-Lago is sixty-five miles from the Everglades. The two are connected by motorway, weather pattern, and a mosquito named Bertha.

This is not a small thing. It changes the texture of the news. When the centre of an empire is in a swamp, the news from that swamp has weight. The Florida Man Daily — twelve rotating headlines we pull from the Tampa Bay Times, the Miami Herald, the Palm Beach Post, and a man named Carl who runs a Tumblr — is not, strictly speaking, news. It is folklore. But the folklore is on the same continent as the foreign policy, breathing the same humid air, and that turns out to matter.

II. The Gator Logic

The Florida Wildlife Commission runs a nuisance-gator hotline. It is a real thing. You can ring up and say there is a small alligator in your driveway and someone will come and remove it. The hotline runs hottest in May and June — mating season — when otherwise-undisturbed reptiles wander from canal to lanai in search of opportunities they cannot, on reflection, cope with. The Gator Incident Index on our dashboard is calibrated to that hotline. Today's top five reports include: a gator in a 7-Eleven in Lakeland; a gator wedged in an Orlando storm drain; a gator photographed at a wedding in St. Augustine; a gator on the eighth tee at Naples; and a gator at a pickleball court in The Villages, where the average age of the residents is sixty-eight.

The Villages is the largest retirement community in the United States. It is also, for various reasons we will not pursue here, the most photographed senior demographic in the western world. A gator on a pickleball court in The Villages is not a story about an animal. It is a story about a country where the centre of political activity, the centre of demographic concentration, and the centre of regulatory wildlife management are now all the same humid 60-mile triangle. What the gator is doing on the pickleball court — and the gator is doing fine, the players were less so — is reminding everyone that the swamp has not been drained. It has just been zoned.

The swamp has not been drained. It has just been zoned.

III. Police Chases as Polling

There is a thing that political pollsters do, on quiet weeks, where they ask Americans an open-ended question about the state of the country and then sift through the answers for tone. The answers, in 2026, do not look much like the answers in 1996. They are angrier, more atmospheric, and more difficult to score. Pollsters have responded by going to focus groups, sentiment analysis, and — increasingly — to what they cheerfully call "vibes data."

The Florida dashboard tracks several signals that vibes-data people would, if they were honest, be willing to license. Police Chase Probability runs higher on Friday afternoons. The Walmart Brawl Index spikes on Saturdays, and especially the Saturday after EBT payments land. The Bath Salts ticker — yes, really — runs on a daily curve calibrated to humidity, weekday, and the moon phase. The Donut Consumption signal is calibrated to Krispy Kreme regional sales as a proxy for local stress. None of these are, strictly, news. Together they tell you something about a national mood that the official numbers cannot reach.

Specifically, they tell you when the median has stopped being the answer. Survey data measures the middle. Live chaos data measures the tail. There is a moment in any society's mood — and we have sat through this in Britain, more times than we'd like — when the tail starts setting the temperature, and the median stops mattering. You can spot it in Britain in the chip shop at half-past midnight on a Saturday. You can spot it in Florida at 11.30am on a Tuesday, with one camera, three cousins, and a bag of bath salts.

IV. What the Cousins Know That We Don't

The serious version of this argument is that Britain is structurally bad at measuring its own tails, and America — by accident of geography and political hijack — has gotten quite good at it. We measure middles. We measure tutting volume, queue length, Stella consumption, kebab traffic. These are honest median indicators of a country that mostly does not kick off. They are useless on the days when it does.

The Florida Index, by contrast, exists precisely to read tails. The Walmart Brawl, the Police Chase, the Naked 911 ticker, the meth-lab bust tally, the gator in the storm drain. These are events at the edges of the distribution, and the distribution is what most other measurement tools cannot, by design, see. Combined, they describe a country whose mood is driven by its outliers more than its median — a structural feature of any nation whose centre of gravity has migrated to its strangest peninsula.

There is a lesson in here for the British. We have been measuring our middle for thirty years and assuming the tails would behave. They mostly have. They will not always. The dashboard's Florida sister is, in one reading, a satirical gimmick. In another, it is a working prototype for what an honest British equivalent of tail-tracking might look like — when the queues finally stop holding, and the chip shop runs out of curry sauce, and the cousins, watching from across the Atlantic, finally feel entitled to a small giggle of their own.

For now, we keep watching the gators. The gators know things. The gators have been doing this longer than the rest of us. The gators do not vote, do not poll, and do not feature in any of the official data — which is, as Florida has been quietly proving for several years now, exactly the source you should be checking first.

They're Coming for the Beans

On the price of a fry-up, the egg cliff of 2023, and what gets measured when nobody's officially watching.

There is a particular cafe — you know the one — where the man behind the counter says nothing as he places it in front of you. Two eggs, two sausages, two rashers, mushrooms, beans, fried bread, slice of black pudding, grilled tomato, brown sauce on the side, fork already on the table. The cup of tea is automatic. The price, until quite recently, was somewhere between £6 and £7, and felt like one of the unbreakable laws of British economics — a reliable line, like cricket in the summer, or the rain in Manchester. Then it became £8. Then £8.50. And in some places that I will not name out of professional charity, £10.

The fry-up has been silently inflating for five years and almost nobody has been writing it up. The Office for National Statistics has been reporting on "food and non-alcoholic beverages" with great honesty and great regularity. They have not been reporting on the plate, because the plate is not their unit of measurement. Nobody's is. So we built one.

The new English Breakfast Index on the dashboard tracks the eight ingredients of a proper full English — bacon, eggs, sausages, black pudding, mushrooms, vine tomatoes, baked beans, white bread — with their pack prices and their per-plate portion costs. The companion card, the Fry-Up Tracker, walks each ingredient backwards through five years of ONS food sub-category CPI to show how each line got here. The numbers are sobering. The verdict line, when the cumulative gets bad enough, reads: They're coming for the beans. It is meant as a joke. It is mostly a joke.

I. The £4.40 Plate

Pull a full English apart at a mid-tier UK supermarket today and the per-plate cost lands at £4.40. That number was £3.12 five years ago, in 2021. The plate is up £1.28, or roughly 41%, over five years — and that is not "above inflation" in some abstract sense. The ONS top-line CPI moved about a quarter over the same period. The fry-up moved nearly twice that. The British breakfast has been outpacing the British economy.

The trick of putting it on a plate, rather than in a basket, is that the plate is something a human being can hold. A 4.3% YoY food-CPI print is a press release. £4.40 is a Saturday morning. It is also, importantly, the home version. Out at the cafe — where you also pay rent, gas, wages, and a small contribution to the new Costa over the road — that £4.40 plate is somewhere north of £8, and rising. The man behind the counter is not greedy. The man behind the counter is desperate.

II. The Egg Cliff

The line on the Fry-Up Tracker does not move evenly. It does what real cost-of-living moves do, which is sit quietly for a few years and then jump off a cliff. There is a calm 2021 — bird flu was still scattered, gas was cheap, Britain had not yet noticed it was hungry. Then 2022 happens. Eggs jumped 22% in a single year. Bread followed at 12%. By the end of 2023 — the peak year, when food inflation overall hit 19.2% YoY — eggs were up nearly 40% on 2021, bread was up a third, and the Spanish growers had run out of water. There was, briefly, a national rationing of vine tomatoes that the supermarkets handled with the dignity of an unprepared Year 9 substitute teacher.

Five years on, the worst offenders are exactly the ingredients you would expect, and the steady ones are exactly the ingredients nobody got worked up about:

There was no Sunday Times investigation into mushroom prices. Nobody wrote a column titled The Mushroom Shock. There were no riots in the Asda fungi aisle. Mushrooms quietly held the line, did their job, and ended the half-decade up a respectable but unremarkable 20%. The black pudding line on the chart is even flatter — the Bury manufacturers, having spent decades being a national in-joke, have apparently stopped being moved by world events. The cured-pork-and-oats sector takes its temperature from a kettle, not from Bloomberg.

Eggs, though. Eggs did the work. If you were going to pick the single ingredient most responsible for the British grocery shopper's sense, in late 2022, that the country had broken — it would be eggs. Bread is the policy story. Eggs are the cultural one. There was a moment, somewhere around January 2023, when the egg shelves at certain supermarkets were genuinely empty, with hand-written signs apologising for it. It is a photograph that will go in the textbooks: a country that had once led the world in industrial poultry standing in front of a refrigerated display unit with a bit of A4 saying Sorry — eggs.

The fry-up moved nearly twice as much as the economy. The British breakfast has been outpacing the British economy.

III. The Bean Line

The verdict line under the Fry-Up Tracker, when the cumulative gets bad enough, reads "They're coming for the beans." The line escalates. There is a calm "Plate's holding" at the bottom and a "This is now a war" at the top, with milestones at "Bacon's being cheeky" and "The beans have fallen" along the way. We wrote the line as a joke. We have spent the last five years watching it slowly become a fact.

Beans are up 37% since 2021. Heinz are up 37%. Branston are up 37%. The own-brand alternative, that dependable supermarket fixture, is up 37%. Beans, like eggs, like bread, are the kind of grocery you don't price-check, because their price has — until recently — been a sort of natural constant. The thing about natural constants is that when they move, they tend to move all at once, and people only notice when the change crosses some unwritten line of indecency. For beans, that line was probably 99p. They are now £1.10. We have crossed it. They are coming.

The serious version of this argument is that there is something profoundly democratic about putting cost-of-living data on the plate where it lives. The ONS basket is real and the index is real and nothing on this site is a substitute for it. But the ONS is not, on any given Tuesday, in a position to tell you that a full English now costs £4.40 at home and somewhere north of £8 out. They do not work in those units. They work in food sub-categories — bread and cereals, meat, milk and eggs — and report cumulative percentages that read like financial data rather than groceries. This is partly fine. It is also a reason that the egg cliff of 2023 happened in real time, was correctly described in the official tools, and was nonetheless absorbed by the British public as a personal grievance about their shop, on their Saturday, rather than as a national event.

IV. What's Worth Counting

If you take the long view, the dashboard's Fry-Up Tracker is doing one specific job: it counts what the ONS does not count, in the unit a person actually shops in, and it does so in public. It is not a substitute for serious economic measurement. It is a translator. The egg line is the same egg line that the ONS reports — the source data is the same, the magnitudes are the same. We have just plotted it on a chart that you can read while you eat your breakfast.

I think there is a small civic value in this. The American sociologist Erving Goffman, who I quoted in the last essay and who I will keep quoting until people stop letting me, wrote that the deepest secrets of a society are not in what it talks about, but in what it doesn't bother to. We do not talk about the price of beans. We do not need to. Everyone already knows. So — borderline indecently — we put it on a chart. Out loud. With colours. With a verdict line that escalates from quiet grumble to "this is now a war."

You can read the chart as a joke. You can read the £4.40 number as a fact about your weekend. You can read the verdict line as either, and probably should read it as both at once. They are, simultaneously, the same thing — which is the point. Britain is measured by what Britain pays for its breakfast, and Britain has been quietly paying more for years. Now, finally, it has a number.

Have a brew. Mind the bacon. Watch the beans.

The Stella Index, the Cocaine in the Thames, and Why I Built a Dashboard for the Vibe

A short defence of measuring the unmeasurable — and a longer one of why every nation should have a national mood-ring, ideally one calibrated to whether its corner shop has run out of curry sauce.

There is a particular feeling, familiar to anyone raised in this country, that arrives somewhere between half-five and seven o'clock on a Friday in late summer. It is the feeling that something is, technically, about to happen. The street has more people on it than usual. Two of them are walking abreast. There are bags. Someone is on the phone in a tone that is louder than it needs to be, and a third party — let's call him Lee — has not yet arrived but has texted "5 mins" a quarter of an hour ago. The pubs are at that very specific stage of fullness: not busy, exactly, but you'd struggle to find a chair without making a friend. A police van has just been parked, casually, somewhere it didn't need to be. And the thing is, you can feel all of this without registering any of it. You just walk into a Sainsbury's Local for a Fanta and emerge muttering, "It's a bit kicking off out there, isn't it."

I have spent some years professionally interested in this exact threshold — the moment when a city, a town, a nation, decides that today is going to be a day. Most countries don't really have it, or have it at a much higher amplitude. America has riots; we have "a few of them have got their tops off near the bus station, lock the door." Italy has politics; we have a man with a Stella who has decided the council are not going to get away with this any longer. The British register of impending disorder is a permanent low hum, almost beneath conscious perception, and every English person carries a small and very accurate detector for it that they do not know they are carrying. We pretend it is just intuition. It is not. It is data — about a hundred ambient signals fused together, processed in milliseconds, and reported as a feeling.

So the question, which a friend asked me at exactly the kind of pub I am describing, was: could you measure it?

I. The Sociology of the Brew

The trick, as Rory Sutherland likes to point out, is that human behaviour is mostly governed by signals so peripheral that the system pretending to govern us — economics, policy, deterrence — does not even know they are there. We do not decide to leave the house because of a cost-benefit analysis about social opportunity. We decide because the kettle has just clicked off. The kettle is the policy lever. If you wanted to reduce binge drinking in Britain, you would not do it through pricing. You would do it by changing what's on the telly at 9 pm on a Friday. The ITV listings page is a more powerful instrument of national policy than the entire Department for Culture, Media and Sport, and nobody at DCMS has ever realised this.

The dashboard you are reading the blog of is, in this sense, less a piece of comedy than a piece of sociology pretending to be a piece of comedy. It tracks roughly thirty live signals — the actual fixtures of clubs whose travelling support is on the Met's quiet list; the Open-Meteo temperature in Manchester and Cardiff; the rolling jackpot at the National Lottery; today's RSS-aggregated tone of the British press; the National Grid's electricity demand (the famous post-EastEnders kettle spike is on this, and it is glorious); the live menu of your nearest Spoons, indirectly, via a model of UK pub-rhythm; the GDELT global news-tone API filtered to UK protest, strike, riot and unrest keywords. None of these signals, in isolation, mean anything. Together, they correlate weirdly well with the answer to the question of whether your nan wants to walk home through town tonight or not.

Some of the signals are entirely live: the BBC headlines, the gov.uk threat level, the police data feed for Central London phone snatches, the Met Office wind. Some are modelled — meaning we have made a careful, calibrated guess based on real published numbers. The Stella Artois Index, for example, is calibrated to roughly 2.5 million pints poured on a peak Saturday across the UK, with hour-of-day, day-of-week, and bank-holiday modifiers. Is this number "true"? No. Is it more true than not knowing? Almost certainly. The actual brewery does not publish its hourly UK throughput, and even if it did, the version on this site would still be funnier.

II. Why a Brit Was Always Going to Build This

You could not, I think, build a serious version of this dashboard in another country. The Americans would put a flag on it and pretend it was a homeland-security tool. The French would put a Gauloise on it and add an ennui meter. The Germans would build it correctly, document it exhaustively in a 400-page PDF, and be terribly disappointed when nobody clicked through. The British genius, such as it is, is in noticing that the threshold between a Tuesday and a kick-off is partly a matter of how much rain has fallen since lunch, and that this is not just a joke — it is, quite literally, the operating model of the country.

We are a nation made anxious by the wrong things and not at all by the right ones. We are exquisitely sensitive to whether the postman has been but blissfully unbothered by the long-term solvency of the state pension. We will form a queue to register a complaint about the queue. We have a national broadcaster that, between 6 pm and 8 pm on most evenings, carries forty minutes of weather forecasts inside thirty minutes of news. The national mood is not really up or down. It is calibrated to something in between, and that something is approximately the average ambient temperature of a Wetherspoons in Doncaster on a Wednesday — a value somewhere around "could be worse", with bank-holiday-driven excursions into the genuinely interesting in either direction.

The British register of impending disorder is a permanent low hum, almost beneath conscious perception, and every English person carries a small and very accurate detector for it that they do not know they are carrying.

There is a real argument, which I will only briefly sketch, that being able to name this national feeling is a small civic kindness. The American sociologist Erving Goffman wrote that the deepest secrets of a society are not in what it talks about, but in what it doesn't bother to. We do not bother to talk about the Stella Index. We do not need to. Everyone already knows. So — borderline indecently — we put it on a dashboard. Out loud. With an emoji. With a needle that swings slowly from BREW at the bottom, through NICKED and ROWDY, all the way up to ALL OUT SCRAP at the top. It is, I think, the first time in British history a 0–100 number has been painted onto the national mood, and the mood has visibly approved.

III. Cocaine in the Thames, and Other Modern Truths

Nothing on the dashboard cheers me up quite like the Cocaine in UK Waters module. It is modelled — there is no live wastewater feed, alas — but the model is calibrated to a real 2019 King's College London study that found cocaine in 100% of Thames shrimp, and to the established weekend-Monday wastewater-purge cycle that European drug-monitoring agencies report year on year. It is, in short, both a joke and absolutely true. The Thames carries, on a Sunday morning, a measurable concentration of the previous night's national misadventure. That fact — that one of the great rivers of Europe is provably cokey — should, by rights, be in every Year Six geography textbook. It is not. So we put it on a dashboard.

I am increasingly of the view that almost everything important about a country is in this register: it is provably true, mildly comic, slightly depressing, faintly inspiring, and the official channels will never tell you about it. The official channels will tell you about quarterly inflation prints and ONS housing starts. They will not tell you that the McFlurry machine at Bristol Temple Meads McDonald's has been broken for nine days. They will not tell you that on a hot Friday in July the kebab shops of Manchester collectively process more meat-by-volume than the average Premier League ground processes burgers across an entire season. They will not tell you that on Bonfire Night the National Grid reports a measurable but small dip in domestic demand at the moment local fireworks displays peak — because everyone is briefly outside, in the cold, watching a wet rocket fail to clear a tree.

These are the things I want my dashboard for. Not because they tell us how to live — they don't — but because they tell us, in a register no other instrument has, that we are alive, in this country, in this hour, in this weather. That is, in the end, what a national mood-ring is for. Some days you look at it and the needle is at 12. Some days it is at 78 and someone has called Lee and Lee is on his way. The dashboard does not change either of those facts. It just notices them, in passing, and gives them a number, and a colour, and a tier name like NICKED or DEFCON BREW, and lets you see that you were not imagining it.

You weren't, by the way. It really is a bit kicking off. Have a brew. Mind how you go.
